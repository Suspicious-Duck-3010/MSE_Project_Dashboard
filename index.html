    The product: the MFC anode (what it is, how it works, how it is made, what matters)

    Built 2026-11-16 from five papers read through a fetch tool (DOIs verified in Crossref). Only the instructor-group paper was read by me page by page; the other four were read partly (the tool truncated long pages), so numbers are tagged and must be checked in the papers before they go into the report. See the reading-status table at the end of this section.

    1. What it is

    • Microbial fuel cell (MFC): a device where electroactive bacteria break down organic matter (for example acetate, or wastewater) and pass the electrons to an electrode, which produces electric current. [Santoro 2017, read]
    • The anode is the electrode the bacteria live on. It holds the biofilm, collects its electrons and carries them into the circuit. [Santoro 2017, read]
    • In this project the anode is a porous 3D lattice cube. The instructor-group paper describes the anode as a 3D topology designed for additive manufacturing, with design knowledge learned from historical anode data. [Chilukuri 2024, read]
    • Typical anode materials in the literature are carbon cloth, felt, brush and paper, graphite, steel, and 3D-printed polymer, resin or metal. Carbon felt and cloth have high area and conductivity; metals such as copper and nickel can be toxic to the bacteria; plain printed PLA is not conductive. [Santoro 2017; Chung and Dhar 2021; You 2020]

    2. How it works (step by step)

    1. The liquid (influent) carries organic matter, such as acetate or wastewater, past the anode.
    2. Electroactive bacteria (the review names Geobacter sulfurreducens, Shewanella and others) form a biofilm on the anode surface and oxidise the organics.
    3. Electrons reach the anode by direct contact (outer-membrane cytochromes), by conductive pili or nanowires, or through redox mediators the bacteria release. [Santoro 2017]
    4. Electrons travel through an external circuit to the cathode, which gives usable power. Protons move through the liquid or a membrane to the cathode.
    5. At the cathode, oxygen is usually the oxidant. The oxygen reduction reaction is often the limiting step. [Santoro 2017]
    6. An ion-exchange membrane is common, but membrane-less designs work with some loss from oxygen diffusion. [Santoro 2017; You 2020]

    The slide 5 picture shows a column reactor with five anode modules M1 to M5, pumps for influent, effluent and catholyte circulation, and sampling ports. The PDF does not explain it further.

    5. What the instructor-group paper adds (most relevant to the dataset)

    • Chilukuri, Song, Kang, Jin (2024), ASME MSEC2024-125075, "Generating optimized 3D designs for manufacturing using a guided voxel diffusion model". It builds new MFC anode designs that are easier to manufacture. [read pages 1-10]
    • Their data: 2,735 anode designs (cell counts 1 and 2), stored as STL and labelled with minimum feature size from simulations. Voxel grid 64 x 64 x 64 with 0.2 mm voxels (that would be a 12.8 mm cube, [Medium: not confirmed that the part was rescaled]).
    • Their design source is reference [12] in that paper: Kang, Deng and Jin (2021), JCISE 21(6):061008. That is the same paper the project PDF cites. I confirmed the DOI in Crossref but have not read it.
    • A surrogate model (ResNet-50 based) predicts MFS with r-squared 0.96; guided generation raised the mean normalised MFS from 0.54 to 0.63 versus unguided. [read]
    • Authors' limits: small dataset; only MFS considered; minimum cavity size, connectivity, resistance and surface area left for future work. [read]
    Discrepancies and hypotheses to check:
    • The project PDF says 1000 designs with a 50/50 split. Chilukuri uses 2,735 designs with a continuous MFS label. Our set may be a subset, with FEASIBILITY derived from MFS (for example MFS against a 2 mm limit). That is a hypothesis [Low]; check against the Excel when the data is opened.
    • If feature sizes are millimetres (1.5-16 mm) rather than the 0.2-0.3 mm struts in much of the CT literature, the CT thin-strut accuracy dispute matters much less. Confirm on our STLs.
    • The x1 codes B, D, P, G, I could be lattice types (D = Diamond, P = Primitive, G = Gyroid, I = IWP are TPMS names). B is unknown. [Low: guess from letters; ask the professor.]

    6. Expert and author views

    These are published statements by the authors of the papers I read, not interviews. I did not find open-access opinion pieces by well-known MFC researchers (for example Logan or Rabaey); those are usually paywalled.

    • Santoro, Arbizzani, Erable, Ieropoulos (2017): MFC output is "orders of magnitude lower" than chemical fuel cells; the oxygen reduction reaction at the cathode is the bottleneck; electrode-bacteria interaction is not fully understood; MFCs sit at a low technology readiness level, and many demonstrations are proof of concept. They call MFCs a platform technology (sensing, energy from waste, sanitation). [read]
    • Chung and Dhar (2021): direct 3D printing of large-scale systems is "not yet feasible"; drawbacks are high cost, low durability, biocompatibility and electrochemical properties; they propose pairing printing with CFD and machine learning, but note a lack of training data. [partial read]
    • You et al. (2020): a fully printed MFC works, but the printed cathodes were the bottleneck, and start-up was slower than for conventional MFCs. [partial read]
    • Calignano et al. (2015): the cathode, not the printed anode, limited performance; long-term use needs an improved cathode; real wastewater would need pretreatment. [partial read]
    • Jin group (2024): manufacturers lack design knowledge for AM, which limits adoption. [read]

    Reading across them: the anode geometry is only one limit in an MFC, and the cathode usually limits power. For this project the useful points are that geometry decides manufacturability (MFS, pores, connectivity) and that printing faults in those features are what an inspection system must find. [Medium: my synthesis]

    7. What this means for the inspection plan (provisional)

    • Likely must-measure features: strut and wall thickness (MFS), pore size, pore connectivity or blocked pores, surface area (derived), and coating or powder residue. Confirm with the data.
    • If MFS is millimetre-scale, the resolution needed from a scanner is far less demanding than for the sub-0.3 mm struts in much of the CT literature, so surface scanners become more plausible for outer features. Interior pores still need a method that sees inside. [Medium]

    The dataset: what each column means, and what drives feasibility

    Analysis run 2026-11-16 on the 1000 designs (Designdata.xlsx plus all 1000 STL files) with offline scripts in the Analysis folder: stl_basic_features.py (geometry from every STL) and feature_correlation.py (correlation, mutual information, rules). The PDF does not say what the columns mean, so the meanings below are inferred from the data. Treat them as hypotheses until confirmed with the professor or Kang et al. 2021. Verify the numbers yourself before they go into the report.

    Main finding [High for the rule, Medium for the meanings]: in this dataset FEASIBILITY = 1 exactly when d1 > 2.0, d3 > 2.0, d2 = 1 and d4 = 1. This rule classifies all 1000 designs correctly (500 of 500 feasible, 500 of 500 infeasible). The smallest feasible values are d1 = 2.05 and d3 = 2.02. That matches the 2 mm printer limit (nozzle and motors) reported for this design family in Chilukuri et al. 2024. Dropping d2 changes only 2 designs (99.8% accurate), so d2 adds almost nothing beyond d4. The design variables x1 to x5 alone predict feasibility with 92.4% cross-validated accuracy (a depth-8 tree), because they control d1 to d4.

    Meaning for the inspection system [Medium]

    • The faults to find are the four conditions: a solid feature thinner than about 2 mm, a cavity smaller than about 2 mm, a disconnected solid, and a disconnected or trapped void.
    • All four are geometric. A scanner must measure thickness and pore size to better than a fraction of 2 mm, and must see whether pores connect through the interior. Connectivity of interior pores is the part surface scanners cannot see directly.
    • The feasible designs are barely above the limit (smallest d1 2.05, d3 2.02), so measurement accuracy near 2 mm matters: a scan error of a few tenths of a millimetre would misclassify borderline designs.

    Not done yet: I did not independently re-measure feature sizes from the meshes to confirm the d1 and d3 definitions. That needs a voxel-based thickness analysis (next step). Only geometry that could be read directly from the STL files (size, volume, area, number of pieces, mesh edge length) was measured.

        Defect detection options (all kept)

        Same seven methods and the hybrid as in the Report tab matrix. The matrix says which features each method can see; the simulation turns that into a detection probability per defect type.

        • M1 Structured-light scanner: outer geometry; limited by hidden surfaces.
        • M2 Laser line scanner: outer geometry; robot-mountable.
        • M3 Photogrammetry: outer geometry; lowest accuracy in the sources found.
        • M4 Contact probe (CMM): exterior points only; slow.
        • M5 X-ray CT: sees inside; slowest and most costly.
        • M6 Ultrasonic, eddy current, thermography, resonance: global pass/fail for some missing-strut cases; cannot locate or count.
        • M7 In-process layer imaging: inside the printer, not a separate cell.
        • Hybrid: surface scan on every part, CT only on flagged parts.
        • Layouts: robot arm plus scanner, turntable plus fixed scanner, gantry or fixed multi-camera, conveyor stations, inline in the printer, combinations.

        How the simulation could be built (outline)

        1. Defect model: pick the batch option above; define defect types and sizes.
        2. Detection model: for each method, probability of detecting each defect type, from the capability matrix, the scan geometry (visibility, incidence angle, occlusion) and sensor noise.
        3. Timing model: a discrete-event simulation of the cell: load, move, scan (per viewpoint), analyse, unload; one or more robots, turntables or stations.
        4. Run the batch through each layout and method set; record the metrics above.
        5. Compare 2 to 3 layouts or method sets on detection versus time; verify the results manually.

        Candidate tools are listed on the hub (Simulation software). A discrete-event library such as SimPy fits the timing model and Open3D or trimesh fits the scan geometry [Medium: suggestion, not decided].
